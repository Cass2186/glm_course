2 · Distributions and simulation

d/p/q/r functions, sampling distributions, the CLT, the bootstrap and Type I error

Course data (loads automatically; click to see the code)

Every test statistic, p-value, standard error and confidence interval in this course rests on one idea: the sampling distribution. Simulating it beats memorising it.

Prefix Meaning Example
d density / mass: height of the pdf or pmf dnorm(0) ≈ 0.399
p probability: the CDF, P(X ≤ q) pnorm(1.96) ≈ 0.975
q quantile: the inverse CDF qnorm(0.975) ≈ 1.96
r random draws rnorm(5)

The same four prefixes work for binom, pois, exp, t, chisq, f, beta, gamma, unif, …

  • Population distribution: the mechanism generating data, e.g. rnorm(n, mu, sigma).
  • Sample: one realised dataset from it.
  • Sampling distribution: the distribution of a statistic over repeated samples. The standard error is its SD, nothing more: sd(replicate(10000, mean(rnorm(30)))).
  • Bootstrap: when you can’t derive the sampling distribution, resample your data with replacement and recompute the statistic many times.

Explore: the Central Limit Theorem

Draw 5000 samples of size n from a population and look at the distribution of the 5000 sample means. The red curve is the Normal the CLT promises.

viewof clt_dist = Inputs.select(["Exponential (skewed)", "Log-normal (very skewed)", "Uniform", "Normal", "Poisson(1) (discrete)"], {value: "Exponential (skewed)", label: "Population"})
viewof clt_n = Inputs.range([1, 200], {value: 5, step: 1, label: "Sample size n"})

Uniform data look Normal by n ≈ 5. The exponential needs n ≈ 30–100, and the log-normal still shows right skew at n = 100. “n > 30 is enough” is a rule of thumb for mild skew, not a law, which is a good argument for always plotting your data first.

Explore: does the t-test keep its promise?

Both groups come from populations with the same mean, so every rejection is a false positive and a valid 5% test should reject 5% of the time. Make the variances and the group sizes unequal and compare Student’s t-test with Welch’s.

viewof t_n1 = Inputs.range([3, 60], {value: 10, step: 1, label: "n in group 1"})
viewof t_n2 = Inputs.range([3, 60], {value: 40, step: 1, label: "n in group 2"})
viewof t_sd1 = Inputs.range([0.25, 6], {value: 4, step: 0.25, label: "SD of group 1 (group 2 SD = 1)"})

With equal n, Student’s test is fairly robust. When the smaller group has the larger variance, Student’s false-positive rate shoots well above 5%; flip it and the test becomes very conservative. Welch’s stays near 5% throughout, which is why R uses it by default.

Exercises

Q1 — CLT by simulation

Simulate 10,000 means of samples of size 5, 30 and 100 from rexp(rate = 1) and overlay the Normal(1, 1/√n). At what n would you be comfortable using a t-test on data this skewed?

Each sample has size n; the theoretical SD of the mean is 1 / sqrt(n).

data.frame(n = n, mean = replicate(reps, mean(rexp(n, rate = 1))))
dnorm(x, mean = 1, sd = 1 / sqrt(n))

At n = 5 the sampling distribution is still clearly right-skewed; at n = 30 it’s fairly symmetric but the left tail is short; at n = 100 it’s hard to tell from the Normal. “n > 30” is fine for mild skew but marginal for exponential-like data.

Q2 — Standard error, three ways

Estimate the SE of the mean of x by formula, by simulating from the known population, and by bootstrapping x. Why does the bootstrap work when you don’t know the population?

se_formula <- sd(x) / sqrt(length(x))
se_boot    <- sd(replicate(10000, mean(sample(x, replace = TRUE))))

All three are ≈ 0.42 (= 3/√50). The sample is our best estimate of the population, so resampling it with replacement mimics drawing new samples from the population.

Q3 — Bootstrap CI for a median

Compute a 95% percentile bootstrap CI for the median body_mass_g of Gentoo penguins (5000 resamples) and compare it with a naive median ± 1.96 × bootstrap SE.

boot_medians <- replicate(5000, median(sample(gentoo, replace = TRUE)))
ci_pct <- quantile(boot_medians, c(0.025, 0.975))

The histogram shows why the percentile interval is preferable to the naive Normal one: the bootstrap distribution of a median is lumpy and can be asymmetric, and the Normal interval assumes symmetry. The percentile method is not universally best; BCa or bootstrap-t intervals often cover better in small or skewed samples.

Q4 — Type I error, empirically

Simulate 10,000 null two-sample t-tests. (1) What fraction has p < 0.05? (2) What shape is the p-value histogram? (3) With SDs 1 vs 4 and n1 = 10, n2 = 40, what happens to Student’s test? (4) Does Welch’s fix it?

p_student <- replicate(10000, one_pvalue(n1 = 10, n2 = 40, sd1 = 4, sd2 = 1, var.equal = TRUE))
p_welch   <- replicate(10000, one_pvalue(n1 = 10, n2 = 40, sd1 = 4, sd2 = 1, var.equal = FALSE))
  • Null, equal variances: ≈ 5% rejections and a flat p-value histogram. For an exact continuous test under a simple null, p-values are Uniform(0, 1).
  • Student’s with the small group having the large SD: about 30%, six times the nominal rate. Swap which group is bigger and it drops far below 5%.
  • Welch’s: ≈ 5% in every scenario.

This is how to check any test or model: simulate under the null and look at calibration.

Q5 — Power by simulation

Simulate the power of a two-sample t-test with n = 20 per group and Cohen’s d = 0.5, and check it against power.t.test().

t.test(rnorm(20, 0, 1), rnorm(20, 0.5, 1))$p.value < 0.05

Both give ≈ 0.34: a study this size catches a medium effect only about a third of the time. A prospective power analysis is just this simulation done before collecting data, and it works for any design.
