8 · Count GLMs and mixed models

Poisson, overdispersion, negative binomial, offsets, and random effects for clustered data

Course data (loads automatically; click to see the code)

Poisson regression assumes E[y] = var(y) = λ with a log link; exp(β) is an expected-count ratio (a rate ratio when exposure is constant or handled with an offset).

Overdispersion, variance larger than the mean, is the Poisson’s usual failure mode (unmeasured heterogeneity, clustering, contagion, excess zeros). Ignored, it makes SEs, p-values and CIs far too optimistic. Screen with residual deviance/df, check with the Pearson dispersion or performance::check_overdispersion(), then fix with:

  • quasi-Poisson: same coefficients, SEs scaled by the estimated dispersion; no likelihood, so no AIC/LRT.
  • negative binomial (MASS::glm.nb): var = μ + μ²/θ, with a full likelihood.

Offsets model rates: glm(y ~ x + offset(log(exposure)), family = poisson()) forces a coefficient of 1 on log(exposure), so the other coefficients compare rates.

Mixed models handle clustering (repeated measures, technical within biological replicates, herds, schools). (1 | subject) gives each subject its own random intercept drawn from a Normal distribution: lmer() for Gaussian outcomes, glmer() for binomial/Poisson. lme4 omits p-values for lmer() on purpose; lmerTest adds Satterthwaite or Kenward–Roger df.

Explore: what overdispersion does to a Poisson test

Two groups with the same mean count (5), generated from a negative binomial with dispersion θ. Small θ means lots of extra-Poisson variation (θ → ∞ is Poisson). Each test should reject 5% of the time.

viewof od_theta = Inputs.range([0.5, 50], {value: 2, step: 0.5, label: "NB dispersion θ (smaller = more overdispersed)"})
viewof od_n = Inputs.range([10, 100], {value: 30, step: 5, label: "Observations per group"})

At θ = 2 the variance is 3.5× the mean and the Poisson test rejects a true null about a quarter of the time. Quasi-Poisson and the negative binomial stay near 5%. Push θ toward 50 and all three agree, because the data are then nearly Poisson.

Exercises

quine is loaded, and blot holds all 48 technical-replicate rows of the western blot data.

Q1 — Poisson and overdispersion

Fit the full Poisson model for quine and diagnose overdispersion.

sum(residuals(fit, type = "pearson")^2) / df.residual(fit)

Residual deviance/df ≈ 12.2 and the formal check confirms severe overdispersion. Poisson SEs are badly underestimated, so the Sex p-value would be far too small. (Don’t read 12.2 literally as “12 times the variance”.)

Q2 — The negative binomial fix

Refit with MASS::glm.nb(), compare the SEs and AIC, and report the SexM rate ratio with a 95% CI.

fit_nb <- MASS::glm.nb(Days ~ Age + Sex + Eth + Lrn, data = quine)

NB SEs are several times larger and AIC drops by hundreds. The SexM rate ratio is close to 1 with a wide CI that includes 1: once overdispersion is handled there is no strong evidence for a Sex effect, although the naive Poisson fit declared one.

Q3 — Offsets for rates

Cities vary 100-fold in population and the true policy effect on the crime rate is β = −0.3. Fit with and without an offset.

with_offset <- glm(crimes ~ policy + offset(log(population)), family = poisson(), data = cities)

Without the offset the policy coefficient mostly reflects chance imbalance in city size; with it, the model compares crime rates and recovers about −0.3.

Q4 — Warp breaks with emmeans

Fit breaks ~ wool * tension and get response-scale marginal means and pairwise contrasts. Which tension gives the fewest breaks for each wool?

emm <- emmeans(fit, ~ tension | wool, type = "response")

Wool A: medium tension is lowest (≈ 24.0 breaks), very close to high (≈ 24.6). Wool B: high tension is lowest (≈ 18.8). Check the pairwise intervals before claiming close means differ, and check this Poisson fit for overdispersion too.

Q5 — Binomial GLMM on cbpp

Cattle herds are clusters. Fit a random-intercept binomial GLMM, read the herd variance, and compare the period SEs with an ordinary GLM.

fit_glmer <- glmer(cbind(incidence, size - incidence) ~ period + (1 | herd),
                   family = binomial(), data = cbpp)

The herd SD is about 0.64 on the log-odds scale. Period SEs are only modestly larger than the GLM’s (period 2: ≈ 0.303 vs 0.291). The main issue is that the ordinary GLM assumes independence and can’t represent between-herd heterogeneity, not that every GLMM SE must be bigger.

Q6 — Western blot with a biological-replicate random intercept

Model all 48 rows with a random intercept per biological replicate. Estimate the share of variance between biological replicates, compare SEs with the averaged lm, and run Dunnett-style contrasts.

The grouping factor is Bio_ID. Bio_Rep numbers restart at 1 in every genotype, so Bio_Rep on its own would wrongly lump “WT rep 1” with “KO rep 1”.

fit_mixed <- lmer(log(Ratio) ~ Genotype + (1 | Bio_ID), data = blot)

Bio-rep variance ≈ 0.00272 and residual ≈ 0.000327 on the log scale, an ICC of ≈ 0.89. The mixed-model and averaged SEs are virtually identical (KO: ≈ 0.03761 vs 0.03762), as expected in this balanced design. The mixed model keeps the measurement-level rows without pretending they’re independent, shows both variance components, and extends to imbalance or measurement-level covariates. Neither approach rescues too few biological replicates or unmodelled blot/batch effects.
