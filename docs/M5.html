5 · Regression diagnostics

Residual plots, leverage and influence, VIF, heteroscedasticity and transformations

Course data (loads automatically; click to see the code)

R runs inside your browser. The first visit downloads R and the packages, which takes 20–40 seconds; after that the browser caches them. Wait for this block to finish before running exercises.

Everyone shows their p-values; almost nobody shows their diagnostics. The assumptions of a linear model, from hardest to fix to least critical:

  1. Independence of errors: broken by repeated measures, nesting, time and space.
  2. Linearity of the mean function.
  3. Homoscedasticity: constant residual variance.
  4. Normality of errors: least critical; large-n inference on β is robust to it.
plot(fit) panel Checks Bad sign
Residuals vs Fitted linearity, equal variance a curve (missing nonlinearity) or a funnel
Q-Q normality points peeling away at the ends
Scale-Location equal variance a rising trend
Residuals vs Leverage influence points beyond the Cook’s distance 0.5 / 1 contours

Leverage is how unusual a point’s predictors are (hatvalues); the residual is how far it is from the line; influence (Cook’s distance) combines both: how much the fit changes without that point. VIF measures how much collinearity inflates a coefficient’s variance; above 10 deserves a look, but never drop a needed confounder just to lower it. For heteroscedasticity: transform (log for positive, order-of-magnitude data), use robust HC3 standard errors, or model the variance.

Explore: one point, three ideas

Twenty well-behaved points plus one red point you control. Watch the difference between leverage (far out in x), a large residual (far from the line) and influence (both at once).

viewof inf_x = Inputs.range([0, 30], {value: 5, step: 0.5, label: "Red point: x"})
viewof inf_y = Inputs.range([-10, 30], {value: 5, step: 0.5, label: "Red point: y"})

Move x to 28 with y on the dashed line (about 17): huge leverage, tiny residual, little influence. Then drop y to 0: the same leverage now drags the whole line, and Cook’s D explodes. A point in the middle of x with a big residual barely moves the slope.

Exercises

Q1 — Read the four plots

Fit mpg ~ wt + hp and write one sentence per panel.

par(mfrow = c(2, 2)); plot(fit); par(mfrow = c(1, 1))
  1. Residuals vs Fitted: mild curvature, suggesting some nonlinearity in wt or hp. Not severe.
  2. Q-Q: mostly on the line, with a couple of high outliers (Toyota Corolla, Fiat 128).
  3. Scale-Location: slight upward trend, a hint of heteroscedasticity.
  4. Residuals vs Leverage: the Chrysler Imperial combines high leverage with a sizeable residual. Check whether it changes conclusions.

Q2 — Hunt down influential points

Flag observations with Cook’s D > 4/n, refit without them and compare coefficients. Should you drop them?

cd <- cooks.distance(fit)

The flagged cars include the Corolla (very high mpg for its weight) and the Chrysler Imperial (heavy and thirsty); the wt slope shifts when they’re removed. Never drop points to improve p-values. Drop them only for a substantive reason (measurement error, wrong population, coding error); otherwise report both fits as a sensitivity analysis.

Q3 — VIF

Fit mpg ~ ., find the VIF offenders, and drop the largest one at a time until everything is below 5.

step1 <- update(full, . ~ . - disp); vif(step1)
step2 <- update(step1, . ~ . - cyl); vif(step2)
step3 <- update(step2, . ~ . - wt);  vif(step3)   # hp still just above 5
step4 <- update(step3, . ~ . - hp);  vif(step4)   # all below 5

disp (≈ 21.6), cyl (≈ 15.4) and wt (≈ 15.2) start above 10, hp just below. This mechanical sequence is not a model-building recipe: keep the variables your scientific question needs, because dropping a confounder biases the others.

Q4 — When to log-transform

Compare price ~ carat with log(price) ~ log(carat) on the diamonds data, and interpret the log-log slope.

fit2 <- lm(log(price) ~ log(carat), data = ggplot2::diamonds)

The raw fit has an obvious funnel; the log-log fit is much more even. The slope ≈ 1.68 is an elasticity: a 1% increase in carat is associated with about a 1.68% increase in price. The log transform linearises the curve and stabilises the variance in one move.

Q5 — Western blot diagnostics revisited

Compare diagnostics for the raw and log-scale blot ANOVAs and check that the Dunnett conclusion survives on the log scale.

summary(multcomp::glht(aov(log(Ratio) ~ Genotype, data = blot_bio),
                       linfct = multcomp::mcp(Genotype = "Dunnett")))

With n = 4 per group both scales look reasonable (Shapiro–Wilk p ≈ 0.77 raw, ≈ 0.90 log); these low-power tests can’t prove normality, so the plots matter more. Both scales agree on KO vs WT, with a KO/WT geometric-mean ratio of exp(-0.84) ≈ 0.43.

Q6 — Robust standard errors

Keep price ~ carat on the natural scale, but get HC3 robust SEs.

coeftest(fit, vcov = vcovHC(fit, type = "HC3"))

The estimates are identical (robust SEs don’t change point estimates), but the carat SE grows substantially: the naive SE assumed constant variance. The effect is so large that p stays tiny, but the inflation would matter for a subtler effect.
